## A Manifesto and an Invitation

*By a practice manager and operations strategist with fifteen years in financial services, now building sovereign infrastructure for independent behavioral health practices.*

---

I didn't trust my SaaS EHR to be around in 2 years. Finding a new vendor is the easy part. Moving from the old EHR is the hard part, especially if they make it difficult to get my data. PDFs, fees, proprietary formats — and that's all for someone who understands data and tech. Most independent physicians and healthcare providers don't.

Data sovereignty isn't a technical preference. It's the difference between owning your practice and renting it. But ownership is meaningless if your data isn't usable when you get it back. Many vendors will "return" your data as PDFs — a 21st century box of paper. Some will deliver it in outdated or proprietary formats with undocumented translation keys. And others will do it for a price.

I've learned that vendors treat your data like their asset if you let them. In healthcare, some EHRs' business depends on that practice. I've learned that data management is complicated but not mysterious. The patterns that solve it in financial services can be applied to healthcare. Some vendors can't give you usable data at any cost, and then you're stuck.

I've spent over 15 years in operations and data management, mostly in financial services — in the largest and smallest banking institutions. I've managed data quality from dozens of vendors into a single corporate system of record. I've been the largest client and the smallest client. I've supported technology transformations and seen products thrive, or get rolled up, or die. I know how vendors treat their largest clients and I know what happens to their smallest ones when something more important comes along.

What I've learned across all of it: you must own your data, because nobody else will ensure it's properly maintained. When entrusting data to vendors — trust, verify, and have an exit plan.

I am now advising a psychiatrist starting a new solo practice. What I see doesn't surprise me: small practices are treated as the smallest of the small by EHR vendors, insurance companies, and every other business in the healthcare space. Revenue and profit are generated through complex tiers of vendors and gatekeepers. They demand data to process claims and payments, and profit from your revenue.

I saw a familiar pattern. I've been the big guy and the small guy, and I know who sets the rules. I also know there's a recipe for independence.

Technology is more flexible and lower cost than most people realize. Flexibility comes from a deep set of standardized tools. Standardized deployment patterns reduce the cost. The challenge is applying the tools and patterns to your business. Healthcare is complex due to a fragmented and layered payer model and equally complex regulatory rules. While healthcare has been dominated by large tech companies — notably Epic and Cerner — the free software movement has created an HHS ONC-certified solution called OpenEMR. OpenEMR is free and receives free updates. A small practice can't run it alone, but the standardized tools and patterns make it more possible to build a team of people who can do it at competitive cost.

Small practices can't afford a large EHR. The affordable products are following the model of cable companies — raise prices, add fees and "packages," and make it difficult to switch. OpenEMR is used by hospitals and clinics in the US and abroad. Like all EHRs, it requires planning and programming to support your clinical workflow. You know your workflow. The tech knowhow to deploy standardized tools exists. I want to close the gap and I'm starting with me.

An outpatient psychiatric practice is an ideal use case because it's simple. I plan to hire tech talent to build to my specs. I wanted a stable UI for the prescriber, and a platform that supports prescribing, billing, and scheduling. I didn't want to use "good-ish" billing and scheduling tools just because they were "included" in my price. OpenEMR includes them in "free" so I can choose other components if they provide affordable capability.

We'll start with a commercial EHR that supports data export. We will discover needs while we build and run the business. First build is a data export capability, and the business process to run it. Once that's a regular process, we will truly own our data with no dependency on the EHR. Next build is to load the data into OpenEMR. That's easy to say and hard to do, so we'll learn about OpenEMR and our data at the same time. Validating prescribing and billing functions is a critical goal of this stage. A likely third build is a custom workflow interface, which will deliver a personalized experience on standard technology. If we decide to accept insurance, claims integration may come earlier. Practice economics will decide that.

This isn't free, but there's a high cost to make it up as you go. I'm sharing this manifesto to see if there's willingness and ability to make it bigger.

A community project could deliver a product for small practices that market economics won't. We can build a modular deployment kit for OpenEMR that would liberate small practices from affordable but captive EHR solutions. This community needs clinical experience to prioritize features that drive value and efficiency. It needs technologists with hands-on deployment and configuration experience to design the modules. It needs cloud engineers and developers to standardize the modules and customize them for specific clinical practices. Most of all, the community needs belief in the mission of improving local healthcare by supporting independent healthcare providers.

I believe independent providers deserve options. I believe an affordable, customizable EHR is possible with today's technology. I believe you can help make it possible.
