# Why Your EHR Might Be Like a Hospital — And Why It Doesn’t Have To Be
A business exists to deliver a product, whether that is retail sales, or healthcare, or an electronic health record. When a hospital is sold to private equity, patient care isn’t the product. Revenue and enterprise value are the product. The same is true for an EHR.

Physicians choose independence to escape decisions driven by others’ policies. Safety and regulatory rules are non-negotiable and payer decisions are financial realities. Staffing, resourcing, and documentation deadlines are choices made by the institution.

Clinics and hospitals operate scheduling, claims, billing, accounting, and collections. They maintain relationships with carriers, clearinghouses, suppliers, and government agencies. They manage technology resources that stitch it all together. Your independent practice must replicate most of these capabilities. EHR vendors promise to do much of it for you.

Your EHR is not a phone. It’s the core of your patient relationships, your continuity of care, your clinical decisions, your revenue, your staff’s job satisfaction, and your compliance. Choosing an EHR is choosing who is entrusted with all that.

Did you choose your EHR for convenience or trustworthiness? Practice management is complicated, simplicity is attractive, and trustworthiness is hard to assess. HIPAA compliance is non-negotiable, and certification is a badge of credibility. The EHR provides a layer you can see and a service contact. That’s front of the house. Back of the house is technology and data exchange across all the other players: insurance plans and claims, PBMs and pharmacies, payment channels, and many intermediaries between. When things break, from a claim to a process to the EHR itself, how does the front of house help you?

The “in a box” EHR is a black box and a problem becomes “a ticket”. Does your service rep know more than “IT is still working on it”? Do they say “we processed the information correctly so you’ll have to contact the insurance/PBM/pharmacy”? Or “we’re resolving infrastructure issues”? Do their responses help you understand when your work can continue?

Your EHR support team should be better than that. They promised simplicity, and you pay them every month. If patients can’t reach you, or prescriptions can’t be sent, or claims can’t be filed, or notes can’t be entered, that doesn’t impact the EHR. It impacts your patients, your workload, your expenses, and your revenue.

Do you know what it would take to switch EHRs? It’s harder than switching between Android and Apple. Will your EHR return your records in a usable format, or do you get a digital “box of paper”? If your EHR is bought by another company, then “migrates” you to the other EHR, who pays for the training and lost productivity? If fees go up like streaming subscriptions are you paying for added value? Switching is a choice. EHRs make it difficult and painful.

They don’t want you to know there’s free software that is ONC certified and can operate on HIPAA compliant infrastructure. It’s called OpenEMR and clinics and hospitals use it today. You probably want to hire someone to run it for you but that’s what those “other” EHR companies already promise. What’s different? First, the EHR software is public and standardized, so more people ensure it works. You don’t pay for OpenEMR, you pay for service. Second, the technology infrastructure is standardized, and it’s the same infrastructure that your current EHR probably uses: AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud. Third, you’ll work directly with a company known as a managed services provider, or MSP, that’s aligned to your interests because you’ll be working close to the owner, and reliability is their product. Fourth, you can and should have your lawyer write a contractual right for you to get your data in a defined format at a time you agree to. The standardization makes this possible. That data export makes it possible for you to choose another MSP or even go to one of the brand EHRs.

Choosing OpenEMR and an MSP is not easy either. The market of MSPs is not large. They may be hard to find. They run steady businesses that may not be accepting new clients. They are not startups looking to sign up as many clients as possible. You will want to check certifications like HIPAA more than you would for an EHR company. You’ll probably have more configuration choices to make than with the “in a box” EHRs. In return, you should have more transparency into what breaks and when: is it with the MSP, EPCS, PDMP, clearinghouse, a carrier, or elsewhere. An accountable MSP helps you identify and resolve the problem. You’re dependent on the MSP but in a different way from an EHR company.

The MSP will be more expensive, but it will also be more transparent. You’re paying costs plus a markup. They don’t pay for software licenses or development, but there will be a markup on the infrastructure. You may have to pay to request custom changes. You’ll have more setup costs, because they’re customizing for your practice and maybe for your specialty. If you’ve gotten data from another EHR, there’s significant effort to understand, map, and load it to any system. While you’ll probably want an attorney to review your contract so that it protects you, remember that EHR companies already have one-sided contracts in their favor.

OpenEMR doesn’t support all specialties, so some workflows may not be as refined as your current EHR. If you trust your EHR to keep your records, you may feel more comfortable with a larger company with a formal support structure. Maybe the economics of your practice support the financial and operational costs and you don’t mind the tradeoffs. And if the open source model itself makes you uncomfortable — who maintains it, who’s accountable — it’s your business, your risk, and your choice.

When an EHR is owned by private equity or chasing an IPO, the company becomes the product to be sold at the highest valuation. Maximizing revenue and reducing costs increase the valuation more than improving the service does. An MSP’s product is its service, and its main financial interest is serving its clients. You will be able to tell when it is not, and you will have the ability to choose a different MSP on your terms.

The only certainties in life are death and taxes. EHR continuity is not on that list. The choice is not easy, but my intention is to expose you to alternatives beyond the most common. I believe OpenEMR and an MSP is aligned with the independence that most practices crave from the beginning. Whatever you choose, I hope you thrive in that independence!

An EHR is like a bundle of services “in a box”. Like cable and streaming services, they offer a menu of options. It’s their menu using their interface. Set it up, and use it every day. It’s also like the phone you use to buy things, manage finances, send email and messages, and more. Android and Apple are very similar. But we rarely switch, in part because switching is a lot of work. For your practice, are you comfortable with your EHR and how confident are you that you could switch if it became a priority?
